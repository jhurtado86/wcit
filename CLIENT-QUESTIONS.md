## Medical accuracy

### 1. Clinical review of the medical statements

A clinician on your team needs to read the service and condition pages and confirm that every
medical statement is accurate for your practice. We kept the wording careful ("may help,"
"promotes healing") and made no promises about results, but the details below are specific
enough that a clinician should sign off on them. Please mark anything that is wrong or that
you do it differently.

**Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT)**
- Each session takes about 2 hours, once a day, Monday to Friday.
- A typical course is often 20 to 40 sessions.
- The chamber is pressurized to two to three times normal air pressure.
- Who it is for: diabetic foot ulcers that have not improved after about 4 weeks of standard
  care, radiation wounds, failing skin grafts or flaps, chronic bone infection (osteomyelitis),
  some serious burns, and arterial ulcers in select cases once blood flow has been restored.
- We may order a skin oxygen test near the wound before starting.
- Who needs a closer look first: lung conditions (collapsed lung, severe COPD), recent ear or
  sinus surgery or trouble clearing the ears, certain chemotherapy medicines, fever or a bad
  cold, seizure disorders, pregnancy, and anxiety in small spaces.
- Side effects: ear pressure is the most common; vision can change temporarily during a course
  and usually returns to normal; serious side effects are rare.
- Patients can drive home afterward. Sessions are physician-supervised. Chamber rules: cotton
  gown; no phones, watches, hearing aids, lighters or batteries; no lotions, perfume or hair
  spray. Most people do not find HBOT painful.
- Your homepage also says "HBOT sessions are usually daily."

**Negative pressure wound therapy (wound VAC)**
- Dressing changes usually 2 to 3 times a week; the pump is worn all day and during sleep.
- It can be disconnected briefly for bathing if the dressing stays sealed.
- Some wounds need it for a few weeks, others longer.
- Used for deep diabetic foot ulcers (including after amputation), deep pressure ulcers,
  incisions that have opened, skin grafts and flaps, select venous ulcers and some deep burns.
- Closer look first: dead tissue, untreated infection or bone infection, an exposed blood
  vessel, nerve or organ, cancer in the wound, blood thinners or a bleeding disorder, poor
  blood flow.
- Side effects: skin irritation from the film, discomfort at dressing changes, uncommon bleeding.

**Debridement**
- Often takes a few minutes, often at weekly visits; numbing gel or local anesthetic is
  available when needed.
- Four methods described: sharp, enzymatic, autolytic and mechanical.
- A little bleeding is normal, and the wound may look bigger afterward.
- Dry black scab (eschar) on a foot with poor circulation is often left in place until
  circulation improves.
- Closer look first: blood thinners or a bleeding disorder, poor blood flow, a wound near a
  large vessel or tendon, significant pain, or infection that needs treating first.

**Total contact casting**
- Cast changes usually every 1 to 2 weeks; treatment often lasts 6 to 12 weeks, and many
  ulcers close in that time.
- Described as the most effective way to take pressure off an ulcer on the bottom of the foot.
- Do not drive with a cast on the right foot; ask about a left-foot cast if you drive an
  automatic. Shower with a waterproof cover or a shower chair; a wet cast is replaced.
- Used for diabetic foot ulcers on the bottom of the foot, neuropathic ulcers, foot wounds
  after amputation and Charcot foot.
- Closer look first: poor blood flow, infection or bone infection, a very deep or heavily
  draining ulcer, fall risk, large day-to-day swelling, or being unable to keep the cast dry or
  come back for changes.
- Side effects: rub sores; the page also talks about keeping blood sugar under control.

**Bio-engineered skin substitutes**
- Applied often once a week for several weeks; application takes minutes and most people feel
  little.
- Used when a wound has not improved after about 4 weeks of good wound care.
- Products may come from donated human tissue (including amniotic membrane), animal collagen
  (pig or cow) or lab-grown cells, screened and processed under strict rules.
- Used for clean diabetic foot ulcers, stalled venous ulcers, select pressure ulcers, stalled
  surgical wounds and some partial-thickness burns.
- Closer look first: infection or dead tissue, poor blood flow, cancer in the wound, allergy to
  animal products, or personal preferences about tissue sources. A sheet sometimes does not take.

**Compression therapy**
- Wraps are changed usually once or twice a week and worn day and night until changed;
  stockings are worn every day afterward, and most people wear them for life.
- An ankle-arm blood pressure test (ABI) is always done before any compression.
- Venous ulcers take weeks to months to close.
- Used for venous ulcers, chronic leg swelling, leg incisions with swelling, and mixed
  arterial/venous ulcers with lighter compression after the ABI.
- Closer look first: peripheral artery disease, uncontrolled heart failure, nerve damage
  (neuropathy), skin infection (cellulitis), or not tolerating compression.
- Warning signs to call about: numb, pale or blue toes, or worsening pain. The page also gives
  walking and leg-raising advice.

**Specialty dressings**
- Dressings are changed anywhere from daily to weekly.
- The page matches dressings to wounds: hydrogel for dry wounds; foam or alginate for heavy
  drainage; silver, iodine or honey when infection is a risk; enzyme ointment for dead tissue;
  collagen for deep or stalled wounds; film or hydrocolloid near closure; barrier creams,
  powders and rings for skin around a stoma; pouching systems for fistulas.
- It lists signs of infection and common allergies (adhesives, silver, iodine, latex).
- "Never soak a wound." "Some dressings are waterproof; most are not."

**Services overview page**
- The "often used for" line on each service, and the order the page gives for a treatment
  plan: remove dead tissue first, then take pressure off or add compression, then dress the
  wound, then add advanced therapies if needed.

**Conditions We Treat page**
- When to see a specialist: a wound that has not started healing in about 2 weeks or has not
  closed in about 6, or any wound in someone with diabetes, poor circulation or vascular
  disease. "A new sore on a numb foot can get worse quickly."
- **Diabetic foot ulcers:** a leading cause of amputation; skin substitute or HBOT if the ulcer
  stalls after about a month; wound VAC for deep ulcers; Charcot foot covered here.
- **Arterial ulcers:** usually on the toes, heel or outer ankle; "punched out" with a pale, dry
  base; pain at night or at rest that eases when the leg hangs down; a blood-flow test comes
  first; a blocked artery is treated before the wound, with a vascular specialist; HBOT in
  select cases; compression can do harm.
- **Venous ulcers:** the most common leg ulcer; caused by weak valves letting blood pool;
  brown, hard skin above the ankle; ABI before compression; compression stockings afterward
  help keep the ulcer from coming back.
- **Pressure ulcers:** usual sites (tailbone, hips, heels, ankles); on darker skin they can
  look purple and feel warm and firm; often deeper than they look; pressure relief planned with
  families and facility staff; wound VAC for deep ulcers; skin substitutes for select ulcers.
- **Surgical wounds:** causes (infection, fluid under the skin, tension, diabetes, poor blood
  flow, smoking, some medicines); see a specialist if still open after about 2 weeks; wound VAC
  helps many open incisions; HBOT for a failing graft or bone infection; total contact casting
  for foot wounds after amputation.
- **Radiation wounds:** can appear months to years after treatment because radiation scars the
  small blood vessels; treating them is one of HBOT's main uses.
- **Burns:** go to the ER first for large or deep burns or burns on the face, hands, feet or
  genitals; see a specialist if not healing after about 2 to 3 weeks; when VAC, skin
  substitutes or HBOT are used.
- **Skin around a stoma:** causes (leaks, poor fit, frequent changes, adhesive allergy, fungal
  rash); a pouch that lasts less time than it used to is a warning sign.
- **Fistulas:** causes (abdominal surgery, Crohn's disease, infection, radiation); some close
  once the drainage is controlled and others need surgery; we work with your surgeon.

**For Referring Providers page**
- Referral criteria, written for clinicians: any wound not healing in about 2 weeks, not closed
  in about 6, or any open wound in a patient with diabetes, poor circulation or vascular disease.
- HBOT sessions are physician-supervised; HBOT is used for diabetic foot ulcers that have not
  improved with standard care and for radiation injury.
- Every plan starts with a physician examination that measures the wound and looks for why it
  has not healed. Patients go home the same day after every visit.
