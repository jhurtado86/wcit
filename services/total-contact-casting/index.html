Diabetic foot ulcer care · Offloading

Total contact casting for diabetic foot ulcers

A diabetic foot ulcer cannot heal while you keep walking on it. A total contact cast takes the pressure off the sore and spreads your weight across your whole lower leg, so the ulcer finally gets a chance to close. We apply and manage total contact casts at our McAllen and Mission clinics.

What it is

Why a diabetic foot ulcer needs a cast

Diabetes can damage the nerves in your feet, so you may not feel a sore forming under the ball of the foot or a toe. Because it does not hurt, you keep walking on it. Every step presses on the same spot, and the sore grows deeper. Poor circulation, also common with diabetes, slows healing further. This is how a small sore becomes a diabetic foot ulcer, and why these ulcers lead to so many foot infections and amputations.

Taking the pressure off, called offloading, is the single most important part of healing an ulcer on the bottom of the foot. A total contact cast is considered the most effective way to do it. It is a snug cast, molded closely to your foot and lower leg with very little padding, from your toes to just below the knee. Because it touches the whole sole and the calf, your weight is spread across all of it. The ulcer carries almost none.

There is one more reason it works so well: you cannot take it off. A removable boot protects the ulcer only while it is worn, and it is easy to slip off for a quick trip across the room. The cast protects the ulcer with every single step.

What to expect

What total contact casting is like

The cast goes on in a single clinic visit and is changed every week or two until the ulcer closes.

  1. 1

    Checking the foot

    A physician examines the ulcer and checks the blood flow in your foot and leg. Infection or dead tissue is treated first. A cast goes on only when the ulcer is clean and the circulation can support healing.

  2. 2

    Applying the cast

    We dress the ulcer, add a thin layer of padding over the bony spots, and mold the cast closely to your foot and lower leg. A cast shoe goes over the bottom so you can walk.

  3. 3

    Walking in the cast

    You can walk, but less is better. Keep trips short and avoid stairs and uneven ground. Keep the cast dry; we show you how to cover it for bathing. If the cast is on your right foot, do not drive.

  4. 4

    Cast changes

    You come back every 1 to 2 weeks. We cut the cast off, measure the ulcer, remove any dead tissue, check the skin, and apply a new cast. Call us right away if the cast feels loose, if you see drainage on it, or if you have a fever.

  5. 5

    After the ulcer closes

    New skin over a healed ulcer is fragile. We fit you with protective footwear, teach you to check your feet every day, and keep following up. Preventing the next ulcer is part of the plan.

Cast loose, wet, or stained, or a new fever? Do not wait for your next visit. Call us at (956) 317-4044.

Who it helps

Foot wounds a total contact cast may help

Total contact casting is made for one job: taking pressure off a wound on the bottom of the foot in a person who cannot feel it. After an exam, our team may recommend it for the wounds listed here.

Dead tissue is cleared first with debridement. A deep ulcer that is still not closing in the cast may also be a candidate for hyperbaric oxygen therapy or bio-engineered skin.

All conditions we treat
  • Diabetic foot ulcers Ulcers on the ball of the foot, under the toes, or on the midfoot caused by pressure and nerve damage. This is what the cast was designed for.
  • Neuropathic foot ulcers Pressure ulcers on the sole in people with nerve damage from causes other than diabetes.
  • Non-healing surgical wounds on the foot Wounds left after a toe or partial foot amputation that keep reopening under pressure.
  • Charcot foot A diabetic foot condition in which the bones weaken and the arch collapses. A cast protects the foot while it stabilizes.

Is a total contact cast right for you?

Who it’s for

A total contact cast is powerful, but it is not for every foot. Before we apply one, a physician checks your circulation, looks for infection, and makes sure you can walk safely in a cast and keep it dry.

A total contact cast may be a good fit if…

  • You have a diabetic foot ulcer on the bottom of the foot
  • The ulcer is clean, with no deep infection
  • Blood flow to your foot is good enough to heal
  • You can walk steadily and will keep trips short
  • You can keep the cast dry and come back for changes

We may need a closer look if…

  • Blood flow to your foot is poor (a cast can be unsafe when circulation is low)
  • The ulcer is infected, or the bone beneath it is infected
  • The ulcer is very deep or drains heavily
  • You are unsteady on your feet or have had falls
  • Your legs swell a lot from day to day
  • You cannot keep the cast dry or return for changes

Items on the second list do not always rule a cast out. Sometimes they mean we treat an infection first, or choose a different kind of offloading, like a removable boot or a special shoe. Tell us about every health condition and every medicine you take.

What about side effects? The cast can rub and cause a new sore, which is why we check the skin at every change. Your calf muscle may get a little weaker, and sleeping and bathing take some getting used to. Serious problems are uncommon when the cast is checked on schedule.

Blood sugar matters, too. High blood sugar slows healing no matter how good the cast is. We work with you and your primary care doctor to keep it in range while the ulcer heals.

Questions about cost or insurance? Coverage for casting depends on your plan and your diagnosis. Request an appointment and our team will help you understand your options before you start.

Questions

Total contact casting FAQ

Short answers to what patients and families ask us most. Your care team can answer anything that is not here.

Can I walk in a total contact cast?

Yes, with the cast shoe we fit over it. But the less you walk, the faster the ulcer heals. Keep trips short, avoid stairs and uneven ground when you can, and rest with your foot up when you sit.

Can I drive with the cast on?

If the cast is on your right foot, no. If it is on your left foot and you drive an automatic, ask us before you drive. Your safety comes first.

How do I shower with a total contact cast?

The cast must stay dry. We show you how to cover it with a waterproof cast cover or a sealed bag, and we recommend a shower chair so you can keep the foot out of the water. A wet cast needs to be changed; call us if it happens.

How long until the ulcer heals?

It depends on how deep the ulcer is and how well your blood flow and blood sugar support healing. Many ulcers close in 6 to 12 weeks of casting. We measure the ulcer at every cast change and tell you how it is going.

Why not just wear a boot?

A removable boot works only while it is on, and most people take it off more than they think. A total contact cast cannot be removed, so the ulcer is protected with every step. When a cast is not a safe choice, a boot or special shoe is the next best option.

What if the cast feels loose, or I see a stain on it?

Call us the same day. A loose cast can rub and cause a new sore. A stain or drainage on the cast, a bad smell, or a fever can mean infection. Do not wait for your next scheduled change.

Does insurance cover total contact casting?

Some plans cover casting for diabetic foot ulcers when certain conditions are met. Coverage depends on your plan and your diagnosis. Request an appointment and our team will help you understand your options before you start.

Related services

Other wound care services

Total contact casting is one of seven treatments we offer. A diabetic foot ulcer often needs more than one of them.

  • Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy

    You rest inside a pressurized chamber and breathe 100% oxygen. The extra oxygen reaches damaged tissue and may help hard-to-heal wounds close.

  • Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (Wound VAC)

    Controlled suction draws fluid away from the wound and promotes new tissue growth.

  • Debridement of Wounds

    Removal of dead tissue to prepare the wound bed for healing.

  • Bio-engineered Skin

    Advanced skin substitutes that support the closing of chronic wounds.

  • Compression Therapy

    Compression for venous stasis ulcers and leg swelling, to improve circulation and promote healing.

  • Specialty Dressings and Ointments

    Advanced dressings and topical treatments matched to your wound type.
