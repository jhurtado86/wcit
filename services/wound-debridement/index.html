What it is

What is wound debridement?

When a wound stays open for a long time, some of the tissue in it dies. Dead tissue looks yellow, gray, or black, and it blocks new tissue, hides infection, and keeps the wound stuck.

Debridement removes it. Once it is gone, we can see the true size of the wound, treat any infection, and give healthy tissue room to grow. Most wounds we treat need it at some point.

The methods we use

Sharp
A scalpel, scissors, or curette removes dead tissue at the bedside. Fastest; used for thick, stuck tissue.
Enzymatic
A prescription ointment breaks down dead tissue between visits.
Autolytic
Moist dressings let your body’s own enzymes clear dead tissue. Gentlest, and slowest.
Mechanical
Rinsing washes away loose debris and bacteria.

What to expect

What a debridement visit is like

Debridement is usually part of a regular clinic visit, not a separate appointment.

  1. 1

    Looking and measuring

    We examine and measure the wound and check for infection. For foot and leg wounds, we make sure blood flow is good enough to heal.

  2. 2

    Numbing, if needed

    Many chronic wounds have little feeling. When numbing is needed, we apply a gel or a local anesthetic first.

  3. 3

    Removing the tissue

    A physician or certified wound specialist removes the dead tissue with a scalpel, scissors, or curette. It usually takes a few minutes; a little bleeding is normal.

  4. 4

    Dressing the wound

    We clean the wound and apply the dressing that fits it. The wound may look larger than before; we have uncovered its true size.

  5. 5

    Repeating as needed

    Many chronic wounds need debridement more than once, often at weekly visits. An ointment may keep clearing dead tissue in between.

Never remove dead tissue from a wound yourself. If your wound looks worse or smells bad, call us. Call us at (956) 317-4044.

Who it helps

Wounds that need debridement

Almost any chronic wound can build up dead tissue. A clean wound bed is what negative pressure therapy, bio-engineered skin, and advanced dressings all need.

All conditions we treat
  • Diabetic foot ulcers Callused edges and dead tissue are removed so the ulcer heals from a clean base.
  • Pressure ulcers (bedsores) Deep pressure injuries often hold dead tissue that hides how deep they are.
  • Venous stasis ulcers Yellow, stringy tissue is cleared so compression and dressings can work.
  • Non-healing surgical wounds Dead tissue along an opened incision is removed so it can close.
  • Arterial (ischemic) ulcers In select cases, after blood flow is checked. Dry black tissue on a poorly supplied foot is often left in place.

Is debridement right for you?

Who it’s for

A physician or certified wound specialist decides when a wound needs debridement, which method to use, and when to hold off.

Debridement is likely needed if…

  • Your wound has yellow, gray, or black tissue in it
  • The wound has a bad smell or new drainage
  • The wound has not changed in size for weeks
  • A wound VAC or a skin substitute is planned

We may need a closer look if…

  • You take blood thinners or have a bleeding disorder
  • Blood flow to the area is poor
  • The wound sits near a large blood vessel or tendon
  • An infection needs treatment first

These do not always rule it out; tell us about every health condition and medicine you take.

What about side effects? A little bleeding and some soreness for a day or two are common. Serious bleeding or infection is uncommon.

Questions about cost or insurance? Debridement is part of your wound care visit, and coverage depends on your plan. Request an appointment and our team will help you understand your options before you start.

Questions

Debridement FAQ

Short answers to what patients and families ask us most.

Does debridement hurt?

Dead tissue has no feeling, so most people feel pressure more than pain. When we expect discomfort, we numb the area first.

Why does my wound look bigger after debridement?

Dead tissue hides the real size of a wound. Removing it uncovers the whole wound so it can heal.

How often will I need debridement?

Many chronic wounds need it more than once, often at weekly visits, until the wound bed is clean. Your physician decides at each visit.

Can I remove the dead tissue myself at home?

No. Never cut or pick at a wound at home; you could cause bleeding or infection. If we prescribe an ointment, use it exactly as directed.

Will the wound bleed?

A small amount of bleeding is normal and is a good sign: we have reached healthy tissue. If you take blood thinners, tell us first.

Does insurance cover debridement?

Debridement is part of your wound care visit, and coverage depends on your plan. Request an appointment and we will help you understand your options.
