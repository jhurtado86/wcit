When a wound needs a specialist

Most cuts, scrapes, and sores close on their own within a couple of weeks. A chronic wound does not. Something is holding it open: pressure on the spot, too little blood flow, swelling, infection, nerve damage, or a condition such as diabetes. Until that cause is treated, the wound stays stuck no matter how carefully it is bandaged.

If you have diabetes, poor circulation, or vascular disease, do not wait for the calendar. A new sore on a numb foot can get worse quickly. And you do not need to know what kind of wound you have before you call. Describe it, and we will tell you what comes next.

What to expect at your first visit

Three signs it is time to call

  • The wound has not started to heal after about 2 weeks
  • The wound is not fully healed after about 6 weeks
  • You have diabetes, poor circulation or vascular disease, or other risk factors, and a wound of any age

Ulcers of the foot and leg

Three ulcers that look alike and heal differently

Most of the wounds we treat are on the feet and lower legs, and most come from one of three problems: nerve damage, blocked arteries, or weak veins. They can look alike, and treating one like another can do harm, so finding the cause comes first.

Where each one usually forms

  • Diabetic: the bottom of the foot
  • Arterial: the toes, heel, or outer ankle
  • Venous: just above the inside of the ankle

Diabetic and neuropathic foot ulcers

An open sore on the foot, usually on the bottom. Diabetes damages the nerves in the feet, so a blister, rub, or cut may not hurt, and you keep walking on it. Nerve damage from other causes, and Charcot foot, can lead to the same ulcer.

It cannot heal while you walk on it. High blood sugar slows healing and weakens the body’s defense against infection, and narrowed arteries can leave the foot short of blood. These ulcers are a leading cause of amputation.

Warning signs

  • A sore, blister, or callus that does not hurt
  • Drainage or blood on a sock
  • Redness, swelling, a bad smell, black tissue, or fever

How we treat it

Pressure comes off first, usually with total contact casting. We remove dead tissue and match a dressing to the wound. If it stalls after about a month, we may add bio-engineered skin or hyperbaric oxygen therapy; deep ulcers may need a wound VAC.

Arterial (ischemic) ulcers

A wound caused by too little blood reaching the skin, usually from narrowed or blocked leg arteries. It forms on the toes, heel, or outer ankle, often small and “punched out” with a pale, dry base, and it hurts, especially at night or with the leg raised.

Skin cannot rebuild without blood flow. Until circulation improves, no dressing can close the wound, and the wrong treatment, such as tight compression, can do harm.

Warning signs

  • Foot pain at night that eases when the leg hangs down
  • A cold, pale, or bluish foot
  • Shiny, hairless skin, or a toe turning black

How we treat it

We test the blood flow in your legs first. A blocked artery is treated before the wound, with a vascular specialist. Once blood flow allows, we use debridement, specialty dressings, and in select cases hyperbaric oxygen therapy.

Venous stasis ulcers

The most common leg ulcer. Small valves in the leg veins keep blood moving up toward the heart. When they weaken, blood pools in the lower leg and fluid leaks into the skin. The leg swells, the skin above the ankle turns brown and hard, and in time it breaks open.

The ulcer is usually shallow and wet. As long as the leg stays swollen, the skin cannot repair itself.

Warning signs

  • Swelling that is worse by evening
  • Aching, heavy, or itchy lower legs
  • Brown stains above the ankle, or a sore that soaks through bandages

How we treat it

Compression therapy is the foundation, after the same blood-flow test to be sure it is safe. We clear dead tissue, use absorbent dressings, and may add bio-engineered skin if the ulcer stalls. Compression stockings afterward help keep it from coming back.

Wounds from pressure and surgery

Pressure ulcers and incisions that will not close

Two wounds we see often in patients referred from hospitals and nursing facilities.

Pressure ulcers (bedsores)

Damage to the skin and the tissue under it from lying or sitting in one position too long. Body weight presses the skin against a bone and squeezes out the blood. The tailbone, hips, heels, and ankles are the usual spots, and the wound often goes deeper than it looks.

It cannot close while the pressure continues. Rubbing, moisture from sweat or incontinence, and poor nutrition make it worse.

Warning signs

  • A red patch over a bone that does not fade when pressure is lifted; on darker skin, purple, warm, or firm
  • A blister or shallow open sore
  • Drainage with an odor, or fever

How we treat it

Relieving the pressure is step one, with families and facility staff. We remove dead tissue, choose the dressing, and use a wound VAC for deep ulcers; select ulcers may get bio-engineered skin. We care for patients from more than 20 nursing facilities.

Non-healing surgical wounds

An incision that pulls apart, keeps draining, or stops healing after an operation, including the wound left after a toe or partial foot amputation. Infection, fluid under the skin, tension on the stitches, diabetes, poor blood flow, smoking, and some medicines can all keep it open.

Once the edges separate, the wound has to fill in from the bottom up, which takes time and the right support.

Warning signs

  • Edges pulling apart, or an incision still open after about 2 weeks
  • Spreading redness; pus or cloudy drainage
  • More pain each day, or fever

How we treat it

A wound VAC helps many open incisions fill in. We debride as needed, match the dressing, and may add bio-engineered skin if healing stalls. A failing graft or a bone infection may call for hyperbaric oxygen therapy; foot wounds after amputation may need total contact casting.

Wounds from treatment and injury

Radiation wounds and burns

Both damage the skin’s ability to repair itself, in different ways.

Radiation wounds

What it is
Skin and tissue damage in an area treated with radiation for cancer. It can appear during treatment, or months to years later after a minor injury or surgery in the same area.
Why it will not heal
Radiation scars the tiny blood vessels in the treated area. With too little oxygen reaching the tissue, the skin cannot rebuild, and dressings alone may not be enough.
Warning signs
A sore in a treated area that does not close; skin that breaks easily; pain, drainage, or exposed bone.
How we treat it
Hyperbaric oxygen therapy raises the oxygen in the damaged tissue and is one of its main uses, paired with gentle debridement and specialty dressings.

Burns

What it is
A burn from heat, chemicals, or electricity that is healing slowly or has become a chronic wound. We treat burns once the emergency is over; a large or deep burn, or one on the face, hands, feet, or genitals, needs the emergency room first.
Why it will not heal
A deep burn destroys the layer that new skin grows from. Dead tissue, infection, and swelling hold it open, and a burn on a diabetic foot is at special risk.
Warning signs
Not healing after about 2 to 3 weeks; growing pain, redness, or pus; fever; white, leathery, or dark areas.
How we treat it
Debridement clears dead tissue, and silver and other specialty dressings guard against infection. Some deep burns benefit from a wound VAC, some partial-thickness burns from bio-engineered skin, and some serious burns from hyperbaric oxygen therapy.

Skin around a stoma, and fistulas

Peristomal skin irritation and fistulas

Peristomal skin irritation

Sore, red, or broken skin around a stoma, the opening made in surgery so stool or urine can leave the body into a pouch. Most often, output has leaked under the pouch’s barrier and sat on the skin. A poor-fitting barrier, frequent changes, an adhesive allergy, or a fungal rash can also cause it.

It becomes a cycle: a barrier will not stick to damaged skin, so it leaks more. Warning signs: burning or itching under the barrier, weeping or bleeding skin, a bumpy rash, or a pouch that lasts less time than it used to.

How we treat it

Barrier creams, powders, rings, and pouching systems matched to your stoma protect the skin while it heals, and a better-fitting barrier breaks the cycle.

Fistulas

An abnormal tunnel from an organ, usually the bowel, to the skin, so fluid, stool, or urine drains through an opening on the belly. Fistulas can follow abdominal surgery, Crohn’s disease, infection, or radiation.

As long as fluid flows through the tunnel, it cannot close. The output is harsh on skin, and losing fluid and nutrients weakens healing. Warning signs: drainage from a scar or a new opening, especially if it smells like stool or urine; raw skin around it; fever, belly pain, or dehydration.

How we treat it

We protect the skin and collect the output with pouching systems and specialty dressings, and work with your surgeon. Some fistulas close once the output is controlled; others need surgery.

Before your first visit

If you have a stoma or a fistula, it helps to bring:

  • The pouch, barrier, and any powders or pastes you use now
  • A note of when leaks happen and how long a pouch lasts
  • Your surgeon’s name and the date of your surgery
  • A list of your medicines

Other chronic wounds

Wounds that do not fit a category

Many of the wounds we treat do not have a neat name. A skin tear that keeps reopening. A cut from a fall still open a month later. A wound in someone on dialysis, on steroids, or with an autoimmune disease. An abscess that was drained and never closed. If a wound has not started to heal after about 2 weeks, or is not closed after about 6, it belongs here, whatever caused it.

Questions

Chronic wound FAQ

Short answers to what patients, families, and referring offices ask us most.

How do I know if my wound is chronic?

Time is the simplest test: not starting to heal after about 2 weeks, or not closed after about 6, means the wound needs specialist care. If you have diabetes, poor circulation, or vascular disease, any open wound should be seen sooner.

Do I need a referral to be seen?

You can request an appointment yourself, or your doctor, hospital, or nursing facility can refer you through LeadingReach. Some insurance plans require a referral from your primary care doctor; call us and we will help you find out.

What happens at the first visit?

A clinician examines and measures the wound, checks the blood flow and feeling in your legs and feet, and reviews your health history and medicines. You leave knowing the cause we suspect and the plan to treat it.

Can my wound be treated if I live in a nursing facility?

Yes. We care for patients from more than 20 nursing facilities in the Rio Grande Valley. Ask your facility’s nurse or doctor to refer you through LeadingReach.

Will my wound heal?

Most chronic wounds can improve once the cause is found and treated, but no one can promise that a wound will close. Blood flow, blood sugar, nutrition, and pressure all play a part. We tell you honestly what we see at every visit.

Does insurance cover wound care?

Coverage depends on your plan and your diagnosis. Request an appointment and our team will help you understand your options before you start.
